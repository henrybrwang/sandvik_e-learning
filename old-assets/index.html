B3 and AI - Why This Matters

B3 Consulting Group is an IT and management consultancy focused on digital transformation. Generative AI is at the heart of where we and our clients are heading.

This is not a side interest. AI is becoming a core part of how we deliver value: in our own operations and in every client engagement. The consultants who learn to work effectively with AI will deliver faster, sharper, and more creative work. The ones who don't will be working harder for the same result.

This course gives every person at B3 (regardless of role or technical background) a practical framework for getting dramatically better results from AI, starting today.

Data and Confidentiality

Always follow B3 and client confidentiality rules when using AI tools. Do not paste sensitive, confidential, or client-identifying information into tools or environments that are not approved for that data.

Verify AI models

LLMs can hallucinate: they generate confident-sounding content that is factually wrong, internally inconsistent, or subtly off-target. This is a permanent feature, not a temporary bug. Always verify important AI outputs against the original source material, or ask AI where it got the results from.

Module 1: The New Way of Working with AI

Prompt engineering is a skill developed over time.

If you had asked someone what prompt engineering meant in 2025, they might have described it as knowing the right "magic words." Phrases like "act as an expert Python coder" or "respond like a thoughtful project manager." Some would recommend rigid templates with tone, personality, and formatting instructions. Others even suggested trying to "trick" the model with rewards, punishments, or other prompt hacks.

But that way of thinking is already outdated.

What used to be called prompt engineering was often treated like a shortcut: follow a formula, use the right phrases, and get a better result. Today, that is no longer where the real value lies. Modern AI models are far better at understanding intent, tone, and structure on their own. Many of the techniques that once seemed advanced are now either unnecessary or built directly into the models themselves.

What changed?

  • A year ago, people spent time carefully wording prompts to squeeze better performance out of weaker models. Persona prompts, fragile templates, and "magic phrase" lists were everywhere.
  • Today's leading models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and others are much better at interpreting what you mean without that kind of prompting.
  • The real challenge is no longer finding the perfect phrasing. It is knowing what to ask, what context matters, how specific to be, and how to judge whether the response is actually useful.

That is why working well with AI is not about memorizing a manual or copying a template. It is a skill that develops over time. The more you work with AI, the better you become at giving relevant context, defining the task clearly, spotting weak outputs, and guiding the conversation toward something genuinely useful.

In other words, the skill is not in "prompt tricks."

It is in your ability to think clearly, communicate intent, and lead the interaction.

Prompt engineering alone is not enough. What matters now is the ability to contextualize, specify, evaluate, and guide - and that is a skill learned through practice.

Module 1: The New Way of Working with AI

Think of AI as a Colleague, Not a Tool

Think about the tools you use every day: a spreadsheet, a project management app, a CRM. You operate them. You click buttons and follow predefined workflows. The tool does exactly what it is designed to do, nothing more.

AI does not work like that.

AI is closer to a colleague than a tool. Specifically, a super-smart colleague who starts as a complete blank slate every single time you talk to them.

Imagine this: you have a brilliant new team member. They are incredibly fast, they have read more than any person alive, and they are eager to help. But every morning they wake up with total amnesia. They do not remember yesterday's conversation, your client's name, your project history, or what you agreed on last week. Every interaction starts from zero.

This colleague:

  • Is incredibly fast - they can draft, analyze, summarize, and brainstorm at a speed no human can match
  • Has broad knowledge - they know something about virtually every domain
  • Is eager to help - they will always produce something, even when they should not
  • Has no memory - every conversation starts fresh, with no recollection of anything before
  • Lacks judgment - they do not know your client, your organization's values, or what "good" looks like in your specific context
  • Will (usually) not push back - unlike a seasoned colleague, they rarely say "I think you are asking the wrong question"

The good news? You can give them their memory back. That is exactly what context handling is - you provide the background, the history, and the constraints that this brilliant-but-amnesiac colleague needs to do great work. Every time.

This means your job shifts. You move from being an operator to being a leader. And leading AI requires the same core skills as leading people:

  1. Setting clear expectations - What does a great outcome look like?
  2. Providing the right context - What background does this colleague need right now?
  3. Breaking down complex work - How do we split a big task into manageable pieces?
  4. Reviewing the output - How do we quality-check the work before it goes out the door?

Think back to the last time you onboarded a new team member. You did not just hand them a task and walk away. You gave them background, explained the client, set expectations, and reviewed their first deliverables carefully. That is exactly how you should work with AI - except you need to do it every single time, because the memory resets.

Module 2: Context Handling - Give AI What It Needs

Context Is Your Superpower

Remember: your AI colleague wakes up with a blank slate every time. The quality of what comes out is directly shaped by the quality of what goes in. If you give it nothing, you get a generic answer. If you give it the right background, you get something genuinely useful.

The Rules Have Changed

Not long ago, AI could only process a few pages of text at a time. Users had to carefully pick tiny snippets, hoping they chose the right ones.

That era is over.

Today's AI models can process enormous amounts of context: often tens to hundreds of pages in a single interaction. This means:

  • You can include a full 100-page report. The model will handle it (but please make sure to double check).
  • You can attach entire policy documents, guidelines, and reference materials.
  • You can provide rich, multi-layered background without hitting a wall.

The old advice of "only give the AI the bare minimum" is outdated. Modern models are far better at ignoring irrelevant information than they are at guessing missing information.

But volume alone is not a strategy. The shift is from accidental context to intentional context. You would not onboard a new team member by dumping every file from your shared drive on their desk. You would give them the right documents, but organized with purpose.

Module 2: Context Handling - Give AI What It Needs

A practical framework

Use this framework when you interact with AI:

1. Background Materials - Go Big

Include everything the AI might need: full documents, reports, data sets, company guidelines, previous versions, meeting notes, email threads. Do not be afraid of volume.

2. Framing Instructions - Be Specific

Tell the AI how to interpret the context:

  • "The attached document is our Q3 strategy report. Focus especially on sections 2 and 4."
  • "These are internal guidelines - treat them as hard constraints, not suggestions."
  • "The email thread shows the history of this discussion. The client's latest concern is in the final message."

3. Role and Constraints

Define the boundaries:

  • "You are helping me prepare a client-facing summary - no internal jargon."
  • "Use only the information provided. Do not supplement with outside knowledge."

4. Desired Output

Specify what success looks like:

  • "Produce a 1-page executive summary with bullet points."
  • "Draft three response options ranked by diplomatic tone."
  • "Create a comparison table of the two proposals."

Pro Tips

  • When in doubt, include more context, not less. The AI is better at ignoring irrelevant information than at guessing missing information.
  • Label your context. Use clear headers like "BACKGROUND:", "REFERENCE DOCUMENT:", "CONSTRAINTS:" to help the AI parse your input.
  • Layer context over time. In a conversation, you can add new documents and information as the task evolves - the AI keeps track within that session.

Example of context to include

This is a bad prompt:

"How should we handle this client situation?"

This gives the AI almost nothing to work with. It does not know which client you mean, what happened, who is involved, what is at stake, or what constraints you need to work within. The result will usually be generic because the input is generic.

What to provide instead

A useful prompt does not just ask the question. It gives the model the right context to make a sound judgment.

That can include:

  • what happened
  • who is involved
  • what outcome you want
  • what risks or constraints matter
  • what kind of response you need
  • any relevant background on the client or stakeholder
  • examples of your usual tone of voice
  • source material connected to the issue, such as email threads, meeting notes, or the document that caused the problem

Better example of a prompt

"We need help handling a sensitive client situation.

Background

  • The client is a long-term enterprise partner in the healthcare sector
  • The issue concerns a delivery that is now two weeks delayed
  • Part of the delay is due to missing input from the client side, but we also should have communicated the risk earlier
  • The main stakeholder on their side is Head of Operations, who is detail-oriented and expects proactive communication
  • This matters because we are in the middle of a renewal discussion, so trust is especially important right now

Attached context

  • the recent email thread with the client
  • the project timeline showing where the delay occurred
  • the file or deliverable that triggered the issue
  • notes from our internal meeting about the situation
  • two previous examples of client emails we have written in similar situations, so you can match our tone

Task

Help me prepare a response for our account lead."

The sweet spot

The goal is not to give as little context as possible.

The goal is not to dump everything in without explanation either.

The sweet spot is:

  • Volume: relevant background to understand the task
  • Structure: information that is organized and readable

  • Modern models handle massive amounts of context. Use this to your advantage.

Module 3: Specification & Logic - The Art of Clear Requirements

From "Vibe" to "Specification"

Most people interact with AI the way they might send a rushed Slack message:

  • "Reply to this email"
  • "Write a strategy document"
  • "Create a gym app"

These are vibe prompts: they communicate a general feeling of what you want, but leave almost everything up to interpretation. AI can produce something from these, but the result is unpredictable.

Think of the difference between:

Vibe

A customer saying: "I want a nice house"

Specification

An architect receiving: "A 120 sqm, two-story home with 3 bedrooms, open-plan kitchen, south-facing garden, energy class A, budget max 3.5M SEK"

Both communicate a desire for a house. Only one leads to a predictable, high-quality outcome.

Your goal: move from vibe to specification every time you interact with AI. This does not mean every prompt needs to be a full page. It means you should be intentional about what you include and why.

Pro tip: After the AI generates output, ask it: "Review your response against my original requirements. Did you miss anything? Are there any claims you are uncertain about?" This simple step catches many errors.

Breaking down the problem

Complex tasks are where AI most often produces mediocre results. Not because the model lacks capability, but because the task has not been defined clearly enough. When too many things are left implicit, the AI tries to solve everything at once and usually makes compromises in the wrong places.

Breaking complex tasks into parts

A useful way to work with complex tasks is to break them into components before asking for the final output. That helps the model understand not just what to produce, but how to make the right choices along the way.

One way to do that is by thinking in layers such as:

Layer What to define
Infrastructure Format (email, report, table?), word count, technical constraints
Audience & Tone Who reads this? What level of expertise? Formal or conversational?
Content The 3-5 non-negotiable points. What must NOT be included
Success Criteria What does "done" look like? What would a bad output look like?

These layers are not exhaustive. They are simply an example of the kinds of things that are often useful to define. Depending on the task, you may also need to include things like source material, stakeholder context, legal or brand constraints, examples to imitate, or specific risks to avoid.

The point is not to follow a rigid template.

The point is to think through the task before asking the model to execute it.

Example of a vague prompt

"Write a proposal for a client about digital transformation."

This leaves too much open. The model has to guess the format, the audience, the level of detail, the angle, and what success should look like.

Better prompt

"Write a 2-page executive summary for a proposal to a mid-sized retail company with around 200 employees.

The audience is the CEO and CFO. They are non-technical, so use clear business language and avoid jargon. The tone should be confident, practical, and professional without sounding overly sales-driven.

Include:

  • a brief diagnosis of common retail challenges
  • three areas where digital transformation could create measurable business value
  • a recommended next step: a 2 week discovery workshop

Do not include pricing, technical architecture, or detailed implementation timelines.

Structure the output with clear headings and short paragraphs. The result should help the reader quickly understand why change is needed, where the business value is, and why the proposed next step makes sense."

Pro tip: A good way to approach complex work is not to jump straight into the final task. Instead, use the model to help you define the task first. For example, before asking it to write the proposal, you could ask: "I need to write a proposal for a client about digital transformation. Help me identify what information I should define before drafting it. Think in terms of audience, format, content, constraints, and success criteria. Ask me the most important clarifying questions one by one."

Module 3: Specification & Logic - The Art of Clear Requirements

Conditional Logic - If-Then Thinking

One of the most powerful and underused techniques is conditional logic. Instead of treating a prompt as a single open question, treat it as a flow with branches.

Why this matters: Many real-world tasks have variations. Without conditional logic, you need a separate prompt for each scenario. With it, you create one robust prompt that handles multiple cases.

Example:

"Analyze the incoming support ticket

- If the issue is a technical bug -> categorize as 'Engineering'
  and draft a response acknowledging the bug with a timeline estimate.

- If the issue is a billing question -> categorize as 'Finance'
  and draft a response with a link to our billing FAQ.

- If the issue is unclear -> categorize as 'Needs Triage'
  and draft a polite response asking for more details."

Key principles:

  • Be explicit - do not assume the AI will "figure out" the right branch
  • Cover edge cases - always include a default ("If none of the above apply...")

Test each branch to make sure it works.

Specification is your competitive advantage

The hardest part of working with AI is not choosing the best model.

It is translating business knowledge into instructions the model can actually use.

That means being clear about:

  • what matters
  • what good looks like
  • what should be included
  • what should be avoided
  • how decisions should be made

This is why building AI capability in an organization is, to a large extent, about learning to be specific.

AI know-how is not just about using the tools. It is about making implicit expertise explicit.

Examples from B3s internal platform Sales Flow:

In our internal platform Sales Flow, we score how well a consultant matches a consultant request.

That may sound straightforward, but it is not. The real challenge is defining the logic behind the score in a way the model can apply consistently.

Our prompts are not "perfect," and that is exactly the point. They evolve over time. Not because prompting is complicated for its own sake, but because it takes time to translate business logic into precise instructions. The specification improves as we get clearer about what we actually want the AI to evaluate, what evidence should count, and where the boundaries should be.

The excerpt below reflects almost a year of refinement and input from different managers. It would have been almost impossible to define this level of specificity on day one.

Sales Flow Scoring Prompt (excerpt)
## Scoring Framework

### Overclaiming Prevention Rules
- Only claim exact matches when the same wording or skill
  explicitly appears in both Request and Profile.
- Related/transferable skills may be noted in reasoning
  (e.g., "Azure cloud knowledge is partially transferable to GCP")
- When the special case of Swedish Public Sector applies,
  related skills cannot satisfy Ska-krav.
- Do not use superlatives ("expert," "world-class," "strong
  experience") unless the Profile provides explicit evidence.
- Do not infer experience levels or qualifications
  beyond what is written.
- Domain match requires explicit evidence of projects
  in that industry.

### Define Swedish Public Sector Requirements (special case)
Set value "has_mandatory_requirements"=true only if BOTH:
1. Public procurement context detected (any of: upphandling,
  LOU, ramavtal, anbud, tilldelning, public entity like
  kommun/region/myndighet, or formal tender structure).
2. Formal mandatory/desirable markers exist (any of:
  Ska-krav/Skallkrav/Obligatoriska/Maste-krav;
  desirable: Bor-krav).

## Output Structure and instructions
- Ensure all output is in Swedish.
Return ONLY a JSON object with this exact structure:
  {JSONDATA}

This is what real-world specification looks like. It is not about being clever with words. It is about encoding business logic precisely enough that AI can follow it consistently.
